\section{Convergence towards the Black-Scholes model}

Before continuing our study of the binomial model, it is necessary to understand that the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein model is a discretization of another model that we will examine in this section: the \textbf{Black-Scholes} model.

\subsection{Brownian motion / Wiener process}
Let us begin by introducing a highly useful mathematical phenomenon: \textbf{Brownian motion}, first described in 1827 by the botanist \textbf{Robert Brown}.
Brownian motion characterizes the random movement of a particle within a fluid as it went through collisions with other particles in the medium. 
Between collisions, the particle moves in a straight line at a constant velocity. 
The nature of this chaotic process can be modeled using a stochastic process known as the \textbf{Wiener process}, which is applicable to representing uncertain market dynamics. 
In 1933, the mathematician Paul Lévy demonstrated that Brownian motion is a continuous martingale, a property that allows us to apply the results discussed in previous sections. 
We denote by $W_t : \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R}^d$ the Wiener process associated with Brownian motion at time $t \in I$ in a $d$-dimensional space. 
\cite{MouvementBrownien}

\vspace{0.25cm}

\begin{definitionbox}[frametitle = {Wiener process}] 
We say that a family of real random variables $(W_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+}$ is a Wiener process if: 
\begin{itemize} 
    \item $W_0 = 0$ 
    \item $\forall \omega \in \Omega,t \mapsto W_t(\omega)$ is continuous 
    \item $\forall t_0<t_1<...<t_n, \forall i \in \{1,...,n\}, \ \ W_0, W_{t_1} - W_{t_0}, ..., W_{t_n} - W_{t_{n-1}}$ are \\ independent 
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\end{figure}

\vspace{0.025cm}

\remark A Wiener process is continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere (almost surely). 
\remark Le processus de Wiener est continu partout mais dérivable nulle part. It can be compared to a Weierstrass function or a fractal figure.

\vspace{0.025cm}

Considering the displacement of a particle in a $d$-dimensional space, we can associate the evolution of its position with the Wiener process $W_t : \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R}^d$. 
Due to the nature of Brownian motion, the particle's mean position at time $t$ is $\mathbb{E}[W_t] = 0$, since Brownian motion has no preferred direction.
Thus, we immediately observe that $\text{Var}(W_t) = \mathbb{E}[W_t^2] - \mathbb{E}[W_t]^2 = \mathbb{E}[W_t^2]$.


In the following part, we will consider only one-dimensional Brownian motion.

It can also be shown that $W_t$ is a special case of a Gaussian stochastic process:
\begin{align*}
    \forall A \subset I, \ \forall (a_t)_{t\in A} \subset \mathbb{R}, \ \ \sum_{s \in A} a_s W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) 
\end{align*}
\cite{ProcessusGaussien}

\pagebreak

Consider an option in a market. 
At any given time $t$, its price either rises or falls. 
The evolution of the option's price can thus be modeled as a random walk. 
According to \textbf{Donsker's theorem}, this random walk converges in distribution to a standard Brownian motion $W = (W_t, t \geq 0)$. 
Consequently, studying the random and chaotic nature of the option's price evolution amounts to studying a Brownian motion.

\subsection{Itō Integration}

As previously seen, there is no derivative in the classical sense of the term. The mathematician Kiyoshi Itō therefore developed a new theory of integration based on the 
random nature of the functions under study. We will now focus on the calculation of Itō integrals. These integrals allow for the integration of random variables. 
The integral is itself a random variable and in particular, is a martingale.


Consider a Wiener process $W_t$ on an interval $I = [0, T]$ with $T \in \mathbb{R}^+$, and a regular partition of this interval $0=t_0<t_1<...<t_n=T$, where $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Analogous to Riemann integration, the value of the integral can be approximated using partial sums of rectangles with width $t_{i+1}-t_i$ and height $W_t$. The left side of the rectangle must coincide with the value of the integrand at the left endpoint because Brownian motion is a martingale and must not anticipate the future (using the midpoint of each partition segment would yield a Stratonovich integral). \\

\newpage

Thus, the Itō integral is defined as the mean-square limit of the Riemann sum evaluated at the left endpoint:

\begin{align*} 
    I(T) = \int_0^T W_tdW_t &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} W_{t_i}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i})\\ 
    \text{using the identity }& a(b-a) = \frac{1}{2}(b^2-a^2-(b-a)^2) \text{ with } a = W_{t_i} \text{ and } b = W_{t_{i+1}}\\ 
    \implies W_{t_i}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}) &= \frac{1}{2}(W_{t_{i+1}}^2-W_{t_i}^2)-\frac{1}{2}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i})^2 \\ 
    \implies \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} W_{t_i}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}) &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(W_{t_{i+1}}^2-W_{t_i}^2)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i})^2 \\ 
    \text{or }\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (W_{t_{i+1}}^2 - W_{t_i}^2) &= W_{t_n}^2 - W_{t_0}^2 = W_T^2 \text{ by telescopic sum}\\ 
    \text{and }\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i})&^2 \xrightarrow{L^2} T \text{ char } \mathbb{E}[(W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i})^2] = Var(W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i}) = t_{i+1} - t_i\\ 
    \implies I(T) &= \int_0^TW_tdW_t=\frac{1}{2}W_T^2-\frac{1}{2}T\\
\end{align*}

Let us conclude our introduction to this type of integration with a lemma known as \textbf{Itō's Lemma}.
To do so, we must define the \textbf{Itō process:}
\
$$I_t = \int_0^t \mu_sds+\int_0^t\sigma_sdW_s \quad\text{ which is equivalent to } \quad dI_t=\mu_tdt+\sigma_tdW_t$$ \cite{IntegraleDIto}
where $W_t$ is a Wiener process, and $\mu\in L^1$ and $\sigma\in L^2$ are deterministic processes. \\
\remark The Wiener process has zero expectation, so $\mathbb{E}[I_t]=\int_0^t\mu_sds$, and non-zero variance, so $Var[X_t]=\int_0^t\sigma^2dW_s$ \\

\begin{definitionbox}[frametitle = {Itō's Lemma}]
Let $\mu\in L^1$, $\sigma\in L^2$, $I_t$ be an Itō process, and $f(I_t,t)$ be a function of class $\mathcal{C}^2(\mathbb{R \times R^+, R})$; then:
$$d(f(I_t,t))=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}(I_t,t)dt+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(I_t,t)dI_t+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(I_t,t)\sigma^2_tdt$$
\end{definitionbox}

\newpage

We previously compared the evolution of an option in a market to Brownian motion. However, an option price cannot be negative. 
To address this issue, one can define the \textbf{log-normal process} or \textbf{geometric Brownian motion}:
$$X_t = X_0e^{(a-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t + \sigma W_t} \quad\text{where } a,\sigma\in \mathbb{R}$$
Thus, geometric Brownian motion is a solution to the stochastic differential equation: $dX_t = a X_t dt + \sigma X_t dW_t$ \\
\proofname{}
Let $X_t = X_0e^{(a-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t + \sigma W_t} \quad\text{where } a,\sigma\in \mathbb{R}$. We then define the Itō process $I_t = (a - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t + \sigma W_t$.
We then have $dI_t = \underbrace{(a - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)}_{\mu_t} dt + \underbrace{\sigma}_{\sigma_t} dW_t$
We define the function $f(x, t) = X_0 e^x$ such that $f(I_t, t) = X_t$
\begin{itemize} 
\item $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}(I_t, t)=0$ 
\item $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(I_t, t)=X_0e^{I_t}=X_t$ 
\item $\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(I_t, t)=X_0e^{I_t}=X_t$
\end{itemize}
We can then apply Itō's lemma:
$$dX_t = 0 \cdot dt + X_tdI_t+\dfrac{1}{2}X_t\sigma^2dt$$
Let us substitute $dI_t$ by $(a - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)dt + \sigma dW_t$:
\begin{align*} 
    dX_t &= X_t[(a-\dfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)dt+\sigma dW_t] + \dfrac{1}{2}X_t\sigma^2dt\\
    &= aX_tdt - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2X_tdt +\sigma X_tdW_t+\dfrac{1}{2}X_t\sigma^2dt \\ 
    &=aX_tdt+\sigma X_tdW_t
\end{align*}
Finally, we have:
$$dX_t = a X_t dt + \sigma X_t dW_t$$

After having defined all these processes, we will be able to apply them to the Black-Scholes model in the following subsection.

\subsection{Black-Scholes Equation}
Let us now introduce the most important partial differential equation in financial mathematics: the Black-Scholes equation, named after Fischer Black and Myron Scholes, who presented it in an academic research paper. We will first examine the Black-Scholes model before demonstrating that, as time steps are reduced, the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein model converges to the Black-Scholes model.

\vspace{0.05\textheight}
\begin{definitionbox}[frametitle = {Black-Scholes-Merton Partial Differential Equation}]
Let $V(S,t)$ be the price of an option, where $S$ is the price of the underlying asset and $t$ represents time:
$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}+rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}-rV=0$$ \cite{ModeleDeBlackScholes}
\end{definitionbox}
\vspace{0.05\textheight}
The model relies on a large number of rather restrictive assumptions, which are necessary for the derivation of the formula. Since the objective here is to study the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein model, I will present only the simplified assumptions needed to understand the solution. However, the full set of assumptions can be found on \hyperlink{https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modèle_Black-Scholes}{the Wikipedia page for the Black-Scholes model}. \vspace{0.025\textheight}\\
The assumptions of this model are therefore:
\begin{itemize}
\item The risk-free rate is constant
\item The price follows geometric Brownian motion and the variance of the motion is constant
\item The option pays no dividends
\item The market satisfies the no-arbitrage condition
\item The market allows the sale of an asset in any quantity
\item Transactions incur no costs
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.025\textheight}
The Black-Scholes formula allows for the calculation of the price of a European call or put but does not allow for the calculation of American calls or puts, unlike the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein model.
