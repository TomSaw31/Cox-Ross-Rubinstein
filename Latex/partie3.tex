\section{Analysis of the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein model}
In this section, we will examine the theory behind the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein model to implement it later in the next section.

\subsection{Objectives and Structure of the Model}

The objective of the \textbf{binomial} or \textbf{Cox-Ross-Rubinstein} model is to find the no-arbitrage price of the derivative product.
The \textbf{no-arbitrage price} is the price at which no arbitrage opportunity exists.
This model is based on portfolio replication, the absence of arbitrage and market completeness to exactly replicate the option's future cash flows and to guarantee the existence and uniqueness of a risk-neutral probability measure.


The \textbf{Cox-Ross-Rubinstein} model is a discrete pricing model unlike the \textbf{Black-Scholes} model which is continuous.
The main advantages of working in discrete time are the ease of modeling and algorithmic implementation.
Indeed, the binomial model is intuitively easy to understand as it represents asset evolution along the branches of a binary tree weighted by the probabilities of price increases and decreases.
We will focus on this representation in the remainder of this section.

To discretize the time horizon of the call, we consider subdivision of the option's validity period into $N$ periods of equal length, yielding the following divisions :

$$t_n=\frac{nT}{N} ,\forall n \in {0,..,N}$$

\remark Letting $N$ tend towards very large values amounts to approaching a continuous model.

\bigskip

For each subdivision, two factors are defined :
\begin{center}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item[\textbf{u}] : the asset's upward movement factor (up)
        \item[\textbf{d}] : the asset's downward movement factor (down)
    \end{itemize}
\end{center}
These two factors correspond to the rise and fall in the asset's price, respectively.
In a tree diagram, we will consider the convention of placing the up-factor on the upper branch and vice-versa.

\remark For the time being, we won't dwell on how these two factors are calculated.

The algorithm therefore breaks down into two distinc phases :
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The propagation phase (Forward Phase) :} Starting from $S_0$ the underlying asset price tree is constructed up to maturity $T$.
    \item \textbf{The evaluation phase (Backward Phase) :} Once the prices are set at maturity, the \textbf{payoffs} on the tree leaves are calculated.
    Then, we go backwards in the tree traversal to determine the payoff $S_0$
\end{itemize}

\pagebreak

\subsection{Forward Phase}
Let us represent the price evolution of an underlying asset with an initial price $S_0$, which has a probability $p$ of increasing by $u$ and a probability $1-p$ of decreasing by $d$ in each period:

\remark The tree is recombining; price paths are invariant under permutations of the up and down factors.
More formally, let $w = (w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n)$ be a word of length $n$ over the alphabet $\{u, d\}$. 
If we denote $|w|_u = k$ as the number of up moves and $|w|_d = n-k$ as the number of down moves, then for any permutation $\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n$, the final price is identical:

$$S_n = S_0 \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{n} w_i = S_0 \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{n} w_{\sigma(i)} = S_0 u^k d^{n-k}$$

This highlights the advantage of discretization. Indeed, for a non-recombining binary tree, the $N=10$ stage would be represented by $2^{10}=1024$ leaves (terminal nodes), 
whereas for a recombining tree, the number of leaves is $N+1$. In short, tree recombination transforms the exponential growth in the number of leaves into linear growth.

The concept behind the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein model (and the Black-Scholes model) is to use portfolio replication to mimic the evolution of a derivative product. 
Since we have assumed the absence of arbitrage opportunities (AOA), the market is complete, and thus any payoff can be replicated, as previously discussed.
Let us model the evolution of a portfolio to establish relationships between the model's various coefficients.
By convention, we will use the \textit{Continuous Compounding Formula} for the discount factor : $e^{-r \Delta t}$ with $\Delta t \coloneq \frac{T}{N}$.
We could also use the discret compounding one $\dfrac{1}{1+r}$ but it is less precise and it will be easier to prove the convergence to the Black-Scholes model using the continuous one.
Let $\Pi=\Delta S+\beta B$, where $B$ earns the risk-free rate $r$. We define the risk-free factor $R \coloneqq 1+r$.
Let $u$ and $d$ be the model's evolution factors, and $p$ the probability of an upward movement.
We consider a call option $C$ whose price can rise ($C_u$) or fall ($C_d$), and we construct our model to replicate this call.

}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
    \node[malnode] (init) at (0,0) {$\Delta S + \beta B$};
    \node[malnode] (up) at (3, 1.5) {$\Delta uS + \beta Be^{r \Delta t}$};
    \node[malnode] (down) at (3, -1.5) {$\Delta dS + \beta Be^{r \Delta t}$};
    \draw[->] (init) -- (up) node[prob, above] {$p$};
    \draw[->] (init) -- (down) node[prob, below] {$1-p$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Thus, since the portfolio replicates $C$, we obtain:
\begin{align*}
    \left\{
    \begin{array}{cl}
        \Delta uS + \beta Be^{r \Delta t} &= C_u \\
        \Delta dS + \beta Be^{r \Delta t} &= C_d
    \end{array}
    \right.
\end{align*}
Thus,
\begin{align*}
    \left\{ \begin{array}{cl}
    &\Delta uS + \beta Be^{r \Delta t}=C_u \\
    &\Delta dS + \beta Be^{r \Delta t}=C_d \\
    \end{array} \right. \iff  
    \left\{ \begin{array}{cl}
    -\Delta udS - d\beta Be^{r \Delta t}&=-dC_u \\
    \Delta udS + u\beta Be^{r \Delta t}&=uC_d \\
    \end{array} \right. \\
    \implies \beta e^{r \Delta t}B(u-d)=uC_d-dC_u
    \implies \beta B=\frac{uC_d-dC_u}{e^{r \Delta t}(u-d)}
\end{align*}
With substitution,
\begin{align*}
    C = \Delta S + \beta B &= \frac{C_u - C_d}{S(u-d)}S + \frac{uC_d - dC_u}{e^{r \Delta t}(u-d)} \\
    &= \frac{e^{r \Delta t}(C_u - C_d) + uC_d - dC_u}{e^{r \Delta t}(u-d)} \\ 
    &= e^{-r \Delta t}\bigg(\frac{e^{r \Delta t}-d}{u-d}C_u + \frac{u-e^{r \Delta t}}{u-d}C_d\bigg) \\
    &= e^{-r \Delta t}(pC_u + (1-p)C_d), \text{ avec } p=\frac{e^{r \Delta t}-d}{u-d} \text{ et } 1-p = \frac{u-e^{r \Delta t}}{u-d} \\
\end{align*}
\cite{IntroductionALevaluationDoptions}
\remark Indeed, $1 = p + (1 - p) = \dfrac{e^{r \Delta t}-d+u-e^{r \Delta t}}{u-d} = 1$. We also necessarily have $d<e^{r \Delta t}<u$


Indeed,
\begin{description}
    \item[If $e^{r \Delta t} \leq d < u$:] One borrows at the risk-free rate
    \begin{itemize}
    \item In the event of a drop: $dS_0-e^{r \Delta t}S_0=S_0(d-e^{r \Delta t}) \geq 0$
    \item In the event of a rise: $uS_0-e^{r \Delta t}S_0 = S_0(u-e^{r \Delta t}) \geq 0$
    \end{itemize}
    \item[If $d < u \leq e^{r \Delta t}$:] One sells short
    \begin{itemize}
    \item In the event of a rise: $e^{r \Delta t}S_0-uS_0 = S_0(e^{r \Delta t}-u) \geq 0$
    \item In the event of a drop: $e^{r \Delta t}S_0-dS_0 = S_0(e^{r \Delta t}-d) \geq 0$
\end{itemize}
\end{description}

In all four cases, an arbitrage opportunity arises, contradicting the No-Arbitrage Assumption. Thus, the risk-free return lies strictly between the best and worst risky returns. \\
The formula can be rewritten using the risk-neutral probability. \\

\begin{definitionbox}[frametitle = {Risk-neutral valuation formula}]
    Let $u$ and $d$ be the evolution factors with $d < e^{r \Delta t} < u$, where $e^{r \Delta t}$ is the risk-free factor, and let $C_u$ and $C_d$ be the future option payoffs:
    \begin{align*}
        C_0&=\dfrac{1}{e^{r \Delta t}}(pC_u+(1-p)C_d), \text{ with } p=\dfrac{e^{r \Delta t}-d}{u-d} \text{ and } 1-p = \dfrac{u-e^{r \Delta t}}{u-d} \\
        &=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}(C_1)}{e^{r \Delta t}}, \text{ with } \mathbb{Q} \text{ being the risk-neutral probability measure} \\
    \end{align*}
\end{definitionbox}

\subsection{Backward Phase}
We can now apply the principle known as \textbf{backward induction}. 
This involves starting at the end of the tree and working back to the preceding node to calculate its value—defined as the expected value of the subsequent nodes—and repeating this process iteratively until the root is reached. 
Due to the non-linear nature of the valuation formulas for calls and puts (involving the \textit{max} function), the values of the terminal nodes can only be determined once the tree construction is complete. 
Consequently, the tree must be built in its entirety before the backward induction step is performed. 
This principle is also highly useful for handling \textbf{American options} and for \textbf{delta hedging}.

Considérons alors le morceau d'arbre suivant :

Using backward induction, the value of node $C_{0,1}$ can be expressed in terms of the values of $C_{0,2}$ and $C_{1,2}$. We thus obtain:
$$C_{0,1} = e^{-r \Delta t}(pC_{1,2}+(1-p)C_{0,2})$$
Thus, we can work backwards up the tree from the leaves to evaluate all preceding nodes:
$$C_{i,t}=e^{-r \Delta t}(pC_{i+1,t+1}+(1-p)C_{i,t+1})$$
where $i$ represents the position of the node among the nodes at depth $t$.
The values at the leaves are determined by the call (or put) formula, depending on whether a call (or put) is being modeled. 
In this case, we have: $C_{i,N} = \max(S_{i,N}-K,0)$, where $S$ is the price of the underlying asset and $K$ is the strike price.

\vspace{0.2cm}

Let us apply the binomial model to an option in the following example:
\vspace{0.1cm}

\underline{Data}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Current price: $S_0 = 100$
    \item Strike price: $K = \$100$
    \item Up factor: $u = 1.1$
    \item Down factor: $d = 0.9$
    \item Risk-free rate: $r = 0.05$, so $R=1.05$
    \item Number of periods: $N=2$
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.1cm}

\underline{Downward phase:}

We calculate the possible paths
\begin{description}
    \item[Time t=1:] ~\
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $S_u = 100 \times 1.1 = 110$
        \item $S_d = 100 \times 0.9 = 90$
    \end{itemize}
    \item[Time t=2:] ~\
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $S_{uu} = 110 \times 1.1 = 121$
        \item $S_{ud} = 110 \times 0.9 = 99$
        \item $S_{dd} = 90 \times 0.9 = 81$
    \end{itemize}
\end{description}

\vspace{0.1cm}

\underline{Calculation of risk-neutral probabilities:}
$$p = \frac{R - d}{u - d} = \frac{1.05 - 0.9}{1.1 - 0.9} = \frac{0.15}{0.20} = 0.75$$

\vspace{0.1cm}

\underline{Calculation of leaf values:} \\
We calculate the call option payoff:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $C_{uu} = \max(121 - 100, 0) = 21$
    \item $C_{ud} = \max(99 - 100, 0) = 0$
    \item $C_{dd} = \max(81 - 100, 0) = 0$
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.1cm}

\underline{Backward induction phase:} \\
\begin{description}
    \item[Time t = 1:]
        \begin{align*}
            C_u &= \frac{1}{1.05} (0.75 \cdot 21 + 0.25 \cdot 0) = \frac{15.75}{1.05} = 15\\
            C_d &= \frac{1}{1.05} (0.75 \cdot 0 + 0.25 \cdot 0) = 0
        \end{align*}
    \item[Time t = 0:]
\begin{align*}
C_0 = \frac{1}{1.05}(0.75 \cdot 15 + 0.25 \cdot 0) = \frac{11.25}{1.05} \approx 10.71
\end{align*}

\end{description}
For this option, the fair price to charge is $\$10.71$.
\subsection{American Options}
So far, we have focused on modeling the price of so-called European options—that is, options that can only be exercised at the expiration date.
The strength of the binomial model lies in the ease with which it can be adapted to American options. Unlike a European option,
an American option can be exercised at any time, rather than only at expiration. One could also examine Bermudan options, which are exercisable only on specific dates prior to expiration;
however, for the sake of simplicity, we will limit our study in this section to American options.
Due to this flexibility, an American option is worth at least as much as a European option. Indeed, an American option offers all the features of a European option
but allows for earlier exercise, thereby making it possible to avoid a significant drop in value—for instance, in the case of an American call option.

\vspace{0.1cm}

Assuming an investor is rational—meaning they will always choose the decision that maximizes profitability—adapting our model becomes straightforward.
One simply takes the maximum of the model-derived value and the exercise value when working backward through the tree. 
Consequently, American and European call options will have the same value, whereas an American put option will be more expensive than a European one, 
because the holder of an American put can exercise the option to prevent the price from falling below a certain level.

European formula: We simply discount the expected value of the next period.
    $$C = e^{-r \Delta t}[ p C_u + (1-p) C_d]$$
American formula: At each node, we compare this discounted value with the payoff from immediate exercise and take the maximum of the two.
    $$C = \max ( \text{Intrinsic Value} \, , \, e^{-r \Delta t} [ p C_u + (1-p) C_d ] )$$
